1. What is fintech, and why does it exist?

Fintech is shorthand for "financial technology" — companies that use software to do things banks traditionally did, usually faster, cheaper, or with a better user experience. The category exists for one reason: banks were built in an era of paper, branches, and slow networks, and they're structurally bad at moving with consumer expectations.

Three forces created the fintech wave:

  1. Smartphones became universal. The phone became the wallet, the bank branch, and the customer service desk all in one. Suddenly anyone could distribute financial services without bricks-and-mortar.
  2. APIs became standard. Card networks, banks, and processors started exposing programmatic access. A startup could now plug into Visa or Mastercard without building its own card network — something that would have taken decades and billions in the 1990s.
  3. Regulatory reform. Open banking in Europe (PSD2), digital banking licences in Singapore, fintech sandboxes in the UK and Asia — regulators began letting non-banks offer banking-like services under specific licences.

Fintech is not one industry — it's a stack of categories:

CategoryWhat they doExamples
PaymentsMove money between people, businesses, currenciesStripe, PayPal, Wise, YouTrip
NeobanksMobile-first banking — accounts, cards, savingsRevolut, Monzo, Chime, Nubank
LendingPersonal loans, BNPL, SME creditKlarna, Affirm, Atome
Wealth / brokerageInvesting, robo-advisory, cryptoRobinhood, Wealthfront, eToro
InfrastructureThe plumbing other fintechs sit onPlaid, Marqeta, Adyen
InsurtechSoftware-first insuranceLemonade, bolttech

YouTrip lives in the payments category — specifically the "issuing" side of card payments, focused on cross-border consumer spend. We are a fintech because we offer card and FX services without being a traditional bank. We don't take deposits, lend money, or run a branch network. We sit on top of card-network rails (Mastercard) and bank rails (a "BIN sponsor" bank, more on this in Day 2) and use software to deliver a better experience than the banks ever bothered to.

Why this matters

The number-one strategic question for any fintech is: which part of the bank's business are we taking, and how durable is that take? For YouTrip it's the FX margin and foreign card-spend volume that banks have historically charged 3–5% for. Our wedge is "we charge near zero, banks charge a lot." If banks ever match us on price, our wedge collapses. (They mostly haven't, and won't, because FX is a big revenue line for them.)

2. The four-party model — who's involved in every card payment

Every card transaction in the world — every tap, every swipe, every online checkout — involves at least four parties. Once you have this model in your head, the rest of payments becomes intuitive.

The YouTrip lens

YouTrip is the issuer (role 3). When you tap your YouTrip card at a restaurant in Tokyo, we're the party authorising the payment against your loaded balance. We earn interchange + FX margin. We are not the acquirer — that's whoever the Tokyo restaurant signed up with to accept cards (often a Japanese acquirer like SMBC or Square Japan).

3. Visa, Mastercard & the networks — what they actually do

People often think of Visa and Mastercard as "card companies." They're not. They don't issue cards, they don't hold your money, they don't lend you money. What they actually are: two-sided networks that connect issuers to acquirers. Think of them as the SWIFT or the AWS of card payments.

What a network does in a single transaction

  1. Routing the auth. When you tap your card, the acquirer's system needs to ask your issuer "is this card valid and does it have funds?" — but they don't know who your issuer is. They send the message to Visa/MC, which looks up the card's BIN (Bank Identification Number, the first 6–8 digits) and routes the message to the correct issuer.
  2. Enforcing rules. Networks dictate the rules of the road: max authorisation hold times, chargeback procedures, what counts as a contactless transaction, what data must be sent, dispute timelines, etc. These rule books are thousands of pages. Both issuers and acquirers must comply or they lose network access.
  3. Setting interchange rates. Networks publish interchange tables — how much the issuer gets per transaction, broken down by merchant category, card type, region, and many other variables. (They publish the rates; they don't take interchange — that goes to the issuer.)
  4. Settling the funds. Networks batch up all the transactions every day and tell issuers how much they owe acquirers. The actual movement of money happens through settlement banks, but the network tells everyone what they owe whom.
  5. Charging scheme fees. For all of the above, the network takes a small cut from both the issuer side and the acquirer side. These are scheme fees. They are smaller than interchange but more numerous (assessment fees, cross-border fees, FX-related fees, etc).

Visa vs. Mastercard — what's actually different?

For most consumer purposes, Visa and Mastercard are interchangeable — they both route payments, settle money, and enforce rules. The differences are mostly commercial:

YouTrip is on Mastercard — this means Mastercard routes our transactions, sets our interchange rates, and provides certain co-marketing budgets. Wise is also Mastercard. Revolut runs both Visa and Mastercard depending on market.

The other networks (briefly)

  • American Express — unusual: AmEx is both the issuer and the network ("closed-loop"). Higher interchange but lower merchant acceptance.
  • Discover — US-focused, also closed-loop.
  • UnionPay — China's domestic network, larger by transaction count than Visa/MC combined but mostly intra-China.
  • JCB — Japan's domestic network. Wide acceptance in Japan, narrower internationally.
  • Domestic schemes — most countries have a domestic network (NETS in Singapore, Bancomat in Italy, Cartes Bancaires in France). Cheaper than Visa/MC for in-country transactions.
Why YouTrip cares about Mastercard specifically

Beyond rate economics: Mastercard provides certain marketing co-funding for travel-card products, access to FX rate feeds, and certain compliance/risk tooling. When we negotiate annual contracts with Mastercard, the things on the table are: interchange rate buckets, scheme fee waivers in growth markets, co-marketing dollars, and access to new programs (e.g., Click to Pay, Mastercard Travel Rewards).

4. Tap-to-pay in 2 seconds: the 12 things that happen

When you tap your YouTrip card at a vending machine in Osaka, the transaction completes in roughly 2 seconds. In that 2 seconds, twelve distinct things happen. Understanding this sequence is the single most important payments-literacy skill — once you have it, you can reason about almost any payments product.

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ THE 2-SECOND CHOREOGRAPHY OF A TAP │ ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤ │ T+0.0s 1. Card emits NFC signal — encrypted card data + cryptogram │ T+0.1s 2. POS terminal reads — captures amount + currency + MCC │ T+0.2s 3. POS sends auth request → Acquirer │ T+0.4s 4. Acquirer sends → Network (Visa/MC) using BIN routing │ T+0.6s 5. Network identifies Issuer (YouTrip) and forwards │ T+0.8s 6. Issuer checks: card active? funds available? fraud │ checks pass? velocity OK? geo plausible? │ T+1.0s 7. Issuer authorises (or declines) → back to Network │ T+1.2s 8. Network → Acquirer → POS terminal │ T+1.4s 9. POS prints/displays approval; cardholder sees "OK" │ T+1.6s 10. Funds are HELD on cardholder side (not yet moved!) │ T+24h+ 11. Merchant batches the day's auths → submits for capture │ T+1–3d 12. Settlement: network instructs issuer to pay acquirer, │ acquirer credits merchant's bank account └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

The crucial insight: what you experience as "the payment" is only steps 1–10. Steps 11–12 happen later — sometimes much later. This is why authorisation, clearing, and settlement are three distinct things, and why a charge can disappear from your statement, reappear, dispute, or refund days after the fact.

Two pieces of jargon to know:

  • Cryptogram — the encrypted blob the chip generates each time you tap. It's a one-time signature that proves the card is physically present and prevents replay attacks. The chip generates a new cryptogram per transaction; the issuer verifies it.
  • MCC (Merchant Category Code) — a 4-digit code that classifies the merchant (5411 = grocery, 4111 = transport, 7011 = lodging, 4722 = travel agency, etc.). Interchange rates differ by MCC. We use MCC heavily in CRM ("users who spent in MCC 7011 last month" = recent hotel guests, prime for a travel CRM journey).

5. Authorization, clearing, settlement — three different events

Three words that look interchangeable but aren't. Getting this right separates payments-literate people from everyone else.

EventWhat happensWhenMoney moved?
Authorisation Issuer says "yes, the card is good for this amount." Funds are held on the cardholder's side. Real-time (the 2 seconds you experience) No — only a hold
Clearing Merchant submits the captured amount to be processed. Acquirer + network match it against the auth. Usually end-of-day, sometimes 1–3 days No — accounting only
Settlement Actual money moves: issuer's settlement account → network → acquirer → merchant's bank. T+1 to T+3 typically Yes — funds move

Why this matters in practice:

  • "Pending" charges on your bank statement are authorisations that haven't cleared yet. They can disappear if the merchant doesn't capture (e.g., hotel pre-auth that gets adjusted at check-out).
  • Authorisation amount ≠ final amount. Hotels pre-auth ~120% of expected bill. Petrol stations pre-auth $100 then capture the actual fill. This auth/capture mismatch is why your YouTrip balance can look weird mid-trip.
  • FX is locked at settlement, not authorisation. The exchange rate that ends up on your statement is the rate on the day of settlement, not the day you tapped. For travel cards this matters — if FX moves 1% between Monday's tap and Wednesday's settlement, the user sees the Wednesday rate.
  • Disputes and chargebacks can be raised up to 120 days after settlement under most network rules.
Real example

You tap your YouTrip card at a Tokyo restaurant for ¥10,000. The Yen→SGD rate at auth time is 0.0089. Auth holds ¥10,000 (~S$89). Two days later, settlement happens. By then JPY has weakened to 0.0086. Your statement shows S$86. Net of FX margin and any fees, this is what actually gets debited. The S$89 hold "disappears" and the S$86 final lands. If you call CS angry that the amount changed, this is why.

6. The money cuts — interchange, scheme fees, acquirer markup

When a customer pays $100 by card, the merchant does not receive $100. Somewhere between 0.5% and 3% gets eaten in fees before the money lands. That total is the MDR (Merchant Discount Rate). It's split between three parties.

Merchant accepts $100 card payment │ ▼ ┌─────────────────────────┐ │ MDR ≈ $1.50 (1.5%) │ └─────────────────────────┘ │ ┌────────────────────┼────────────────────┐ ▼ ▼ ▼ ┌─────────────┐ ┌─────────────┐ ┌─────────────┐ │ Interchange │ │ Scheme fee │ │ Acquirer │ │ ~1.10–1.30% │ │ ~0.10–0.15% │ │ markup │ │ │ │ │ │ ~0.10–0.30% │ ├─────────────┤ ├─────────────┤ ├─────────────┤ │ Goes to │ │ Goes to │ │ Goes to │ │ ISSUER │ │ NETWORK │ │ ACQUIRER │ │ (YouTrip) │ │ (Mastercard)│ │ (e.g. │ │ │ │ │ │ Adyen) │ └─────────────┘ └─────────────┘ └─────────────┘

Interchange is the biggest piece, and it flows from acquirer to issuer (i.e., the issuer is paid by the acquirer's side of the transaction). It's the fundamental reason issuing is a business at all.

Interchange varies enormously by:

  • Region. EU interchange is regulated to 0.2% (debit) / 0.3% (credit). US is mostly unregulated at ~1.5–2.5%. APAC varies — Singapore averages ~0.6–1.0%, Australia regulated similar to EU.
  • Card type. Consumer debit < consumer credit < premium credit < commercial.
  • MCC. Supermarkets pay less than restaurants. Petrol stations pay less than airlines.
  • Cross-border or domestic. Cross-border transactions earn higher interchange than domestic ones. This is hugely important for YouTrip — our customers spend abroad, so we earn cross-border interchange which is materially better than domestic.
  • Card-present (in-person tap) vs. card-not-present (online). Card-not-present pays more — higher fraud risk.

Scheme fees (network fees) are small but plentiful. Mastercard publishes about 60 different fee categories. They sum to roughly 0.10–0.15% per transaction, plus some flat per-message fees. They go to the network.

Acquirer markup is what the merchant's processor (Stripe, Adyen, Razer, etc.) adds on top. It's their margin for connecting the merchant. Tiny merchants pay more (3% all-in), big merchants negotiate down (~0.5% above interchange+scheme).

7. "Take rate" — the single most important number in fintech

If you take only one term from this entire document, take this one.

Definition

Take rate = revenue ÷ gross transaction volume (GTV).
It's the percentage of money flowing through your platform that you keep as revenue.

Take rate is the universal yardstick for any platform that moves money or volume — payments, marketplaces, ride-hailing, BNPL, anything. It answers the question: for every $100 of activity, how much is mine?

Visa / Mastercard (network)Route transactions~0.13–0.15%
Stripe (acquirer)Acquiring + payment processing~1.5–2.9% (charged) / ~0.4% net margin

ShopifyE-commerce platform + payments~2.5% (blended)

YouTrip's 3.4% take rate is high for a card business. The norm for a pure-issuing operation is closer to 1.5–2.0% (interchange alone). The reason we earn 3.4% is that we stack two revenue streams on every transaction:

  1. Cross-border interchange — roughly 1.0–1.5% (varies by region and MCC).
  2. FX margin — roughly 1.5–2.0% on the currency conversion, baked into the rate we show the customer.

(Plus a small amount of float interest and ancillary fees, but those are rounding errors.) The full breakdown sits in Day 2, section 7 — but lock in the headline now: 3.4% take rate. For every $100 a YouTrip user spends abroad, we earn $3.40.

Why take rate, not revenue?

Revenue tells you scale; take rate tells you quality. A business doing $1B GTV at 0.5% take rate ($5M revenue) is in trouble. A business doing $100M GTV at 5% take rate ($5M revenue) is in great shape — same revenue, but the higher take rate is a moat. Wise is "low take rate, transparent"; YouTrip is "higher take rate, premium UX." Both are valid; both have different defensibility profiles. The whole strategic conversation at YouTrip is: how do we grow GTV without compromising take rate?

8. FX in payments — DCC, markups, mid-market

When you spend in a currency that isn't your card's home currency, an exchange happens. There are three rates worth knowing.

RateWhat it isWho quotes it
Mid-market rateThe "true" rate — midpoint between buy and sell on the wholesale market. What you see on Google or XE.Interbank market. Not directly available to consumers.
Network rateThe rate Visa or Mastercard publishes daily for settling transactions. Typically ~0.2–0.5% off mid-market.Visa/Mastercard (different for each).
Customer rateWhat the customer is actually charged. Network rate + issuer's FX margin.The issuer (e.g., YouTrip, your bank).

Banks typically charge customers 2–4% above the network rate on foreign spend. YouTrip charges close to 0% above the network rate during weekdays — that's the "0% FX fee" promise. The reason we can do this and still earn money is the network rate itself sits just inside mid-market (Mastercard takes its margin there), plus we capture small additional spreads at weekends and on certain currency pairs.

DCC (Dynamic Currency Conversion) is a scam you should know about. When you tap your YouTrip card at a Bali hotel, the terminal might ask: "Pay in IDR (local) or SGD (your home)?" If you choose SGD, the hotel's acquirer does the conversion — at their rate, which is typically 3–7% worse than what YouTrip would give you. Always choose the local currency. YouTrip earns nothing if you accept DCC; the merchant's acquirer does. This is one of the things our marketing actively educates customers about.

Memorise this

The cardinal rule of cross-border spending: always pay in the local currency. If the terminal offers you "pay in SGD" while you're in Tokyo, hit decline and pay in JPY. DCC takes 3–7% out of your wallet and gives it to the merchant's acquirer. It's the most expensive mistake travellers make.

It's possible to be both issuer and acquirer (Adyen does both; Revolut is doing both increasingly), but they're different organisational muscles. Most fintechs pick one side and go deep.

YouTrip is issuer-only. We do not sign up merchants. We do not run the acquiring side of our customers' transactions. We sit on the cardholder side of every transaction and earn interchange from acquirers.

Why issuer-only is a focused bet

The issuing side of cross-border travel has a clearer wedge against incumbents (banks charge 3–5% FX, we charge ~0%). Acquiring is a tougher game — Stripe/Adyen already do it well, and merchant CAC is brutal. YouTrip's strategic clarity is: be the best travel issuer in Asia. Don't get distracted into merchant acquiring.

2. Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com — what acquirers really do

You'll hear "Stripe" thrown around casually. Worth understanding what they actually do — both because it's the other half of payments, and because the techniques they pioneered (developer-first onboarding, transparent pricing, API-first stack) inform how fintech is built generally.

A modern acquirer / payment service provider (PSP) does five things:

  1. Merchant onboarding. Sign up the business: KYB (Know Your Business), bank verification, MCC assignment, risk profiling.
  2. Payment acceptance. Take card data (chip, online, wallet, etc.) and submit auth requests to the network. Handle 3D Secure where required.
  3. Routing & smart processing. Where multiple acquirer relationships exist, route each transaction to the one most likely to approve and at the lowest cost.
  4. Settlement. Receive funds from the network, deduct fees, payout to the merchant's bank (usually T+2 to T+7).
  5. Risk & chargebacks. Detect fraudulent merchants, manage disputes, handle the network's chargeback procedures, refunds, etc.

3. Apple Pay & Google Pay — tokenization, not a new network

The most common misconception in fintech: thinking Apple Pay is a payment network. It isn't. Apple Pay and Google Pay are wallets. They don't move money; they tokenize cards. The underlying transaction still goes Visa/Mastercard → issuer → acquirer, exactly like a plastic-card tap.

What happens when you provision a card to Apple Pay

  1. You open Apple Wallet → "Add Card" → enter card details (or scan).
  2. Apple's servers contact the network (Visa or MC) and request a DPAN (Device Primary Account Number) — a tokenized version of your real card number, scoped to your specific device.
  3. The network asks the issuer (YouTrip) "is this card eligible? Approve this tokenisation request?"
  4. If approved, the network creates a token mapping: DPAN ↔ real PAN. The DPAN is stored in the device's Secure Element (hardware-level encrypted chip).
  5. When you tap, the device transmits the DPAN — not your real card number — plus a one-time cryptogram.
  6. The acquirer routes the transaction to the network. The network looks up the token, swaps it for the real PAN, and forwards to the issuer.
  7. From the issuer's POV, it's the same transaction as a plastic-card tap, with one extra signal: "this came via Apple Pay" (the issuer can see token type).

The crucial implication: Apple Pay does not earn interchange. Interchange still goes to the issuer (YouTrip). Apple charges the issuer a small fee for provisioning each card (roughly 0.15% of the user's spend, depending on negotiation) — that's where Apple's revenue from Apple Pay comes from. Google Pay charges issuers nothing.

Why this matters

  • Tokenisation is a security upgrade. The DPAN is useless outside that one device. Apple Pay transactions have ~5× lower fraud rates than plastic-card transactions.
  • Apple Pay provisioning is a high-intent signal. A user who provisions a card to Apple Pay is significantly more likely to be active. We track wallet provisioning as a key activation event (table RAW_WALLET_TOKEN in BigQuery).
  • Apple has structural power. Because Apple controls the device + the wallet + the Secure Element, they can extract economic rent from issuers (the 0.15% fee). Banks have spent years trying to negotiate it down. Mostly unsuccessfully.
  • Google Pay is more open — runs on Android, doesn't charge issuers, but doesn't have the same brand pull as Apple Pay.
Regional note

In China, neither Apple Pay nor Google Pay matter much — Alipay and WeChat Pay dominate, running on QR-code rails outside the card networks entirely. In Singapore, Apple Pay is widely used; PayNow (QR + bank transfer rails, not card) is also dominant for peer-to-peer and low-value payments. Travel cards like YouTrip are spent abroad, where card rails still dominate — so the wallet/QR question matters less to us than to a domestic Singapore card.

3DS in one paragraph

When you check out online and a "verify with your phone" pop-up appears, you're going through 3DS. The issuer (us) gets to challenge the cardholder for additional auth before approving. If the merchant invokes 3DS and the user passes, liability for fraud shifts from the merchant to the issuer. Most online merchants now invoke 3DS by default. Cardholders hate it (friction), regulators love it (PSD2 in Europe basically mandates it). The current standard is 3DS 2.x, which can be silent (risk-scored) instead of always-challenge.

KYC at YouTrip in one paragraph

When a user signs up on the YouTrip app, they don't have an active card yet. They have to submit identity documents (NRIC + selfie in SG; passport elsewhere) and we run them through KYC checks — name match, document validity, sanctions screening, PEP (Politically Exposed Person) check, address verification. If they pass, the user becomes "approved" — they can load funds and start spending. CPApproved (cost per approved user) is therefore our most important paid-marketing metric — it's the cost of producing a user who has actually cleared the regulatory gate and can transact. (Sign-ups who never complete KYC are wasted spend; we don't celebrate them.)

AML in one paragraph

Once a user is active, we have to keep watching them. Transaction monitoring rules look for patterns: structuring (many small loads just under reporting thresholds), unusual geo (card used in 4 countries in one day), high-value transactions, sudden behaviour change. Hits get reviewed; some get reported to the MAS via a STR (Suspicious Transaction Report). AML failures are existential risk for any fintech — fines run into hundreds of millions, and the regulator can pull our licence.

PCI DSS in one paragraph

Anyone who touches raw card numbers is required to be PCI-compliant. There are four levels based on transaction volume; YouTrip is Level 1 (the strictest). In practice, this means we never store raw PANs (we tokenise everything), we segregate the card-data systems from everything else, we encrypt everything at rest and in transit, and we get audited annually. The reason Stripe and similar PSPs are so popular is they take the PCI burden off the merchant — if you use Stripe Elements, you're not storing card data, so you only need the lightest PCI tier.

5. BIN sponsorship — how non-banks issue cards

Here's the question every fintech-curious person asks: if YouTrip is not a bank, how is it allowed to issue cards?

The answer: BIN sponsorship. The card networks (Visa, Mastercard) only license BINs — the first 6–8 digits of a card number — to licensed financial institutions. So if you're not a bank, you can't get your own BIN directly. What you can do is partner with a bank that does have a BIN, and operate under their licence. They sponsor your card program; you do the consumer-facing product.

[ NETWORK ] [ NETWORK ] │ │ │ licences BIN │ licences BIN ▼ ▼ [ BANK X ] [ BANK Y ] │ │ │ sponsors │ sponsors ▼ ▼ [ FINTECH A ] [ FINTECH B ] (issues cards (issues cards under Bank X's under Bank Y's licence) licence)

There are two flavours of this relationship:

  • Principal member — the entity holds the network licence directly and bears all the rule-compliance, settlement, and reporting obligations to the network. Big banks are usually principal members.
  • Affiliate (or "sponsored") member — operates under a principal member's umbrella. The principal carries the formal compliance, the affiliate runs the product. Most fintechs operate this way until they reach scale.

What does the sponsor bank get out of it?

  • A cut of interchange (typically a sleeve of 5–15% of the fintech's gross interchange).
  • Monthly platform / sponsorship fees.
  • Compliance oversight obligations (they're on the hook to the regulator if the fintech screws up).

The big consequence for fintechs: your BIN sponsor is a single point of failure. If they pull the plug, your card program dies — every card stops working overnight. Wirecard's collapse in 2020 stranded several fintechs because Wirecard had been their sponsor. Picking a stable BIN sponsor and having a contingency plan is a core ops responsibility for every issuing fintech.

7. How YouTrip makes money — the 3.4% take rate broken down

Day 1 told you the headline: 3.4% take rate. Now we'll decompose it.

Revenue streamApprox contribution to take rateWhat drives it

2. FX margin ~1.5–1.8% Spread baked into our conversion rate. Bigger on exotic currency pairs, smaller on major ones.
3. Weekend/holiday FX surcharge ~0.05–0.1% We don't charge consumers — but we do capture a tiny additional spread on weekend rates the network publishes.
4. ATM withdrawal economics ~0.05–0.1% ATM operators pay interchange to issuers on cash withdrawals. Net of any operator fees we absorb.
5. Float income ~0.1–0.2% Interest earned on the float (money loaded on cards but not yet spent). Higher when interest rates are high.
6. Ancillary (top-up fees, premium tier, etc.) ~0.05% Card replacement fees, future premium tiers, partner referrals.

(The exact percentages shift by market and quarter — these are directional shares to help you reason about the business. Numbers 1 and 2 are the dominant pieces; together they're roughly 90% of our revenue.)

Subtractions: what comes out of that 3.4%

3.4% take rate is gross. To get to net revenue (what flows to YouTrip's P&L after the costs of being in the payment business), subtract:

  • BIN sponsor sleeve — small % of interchange goes to the sponsor bank
  • Scheme fees — Mastercard's small per-transaction charges
  • Card production + delivery — physical card cost (~$2 per card)
  • Tokenisation fees — Apple Pay's ~0.15% per provisioned-card transaction
  • Fraud losses — typically <0.1% of GTV for a clean travel-card book
  • Processing infrastructure — issuing processor (Marqeta or similar) per-txn fees

Net contribution margin per transaction is meaningfully lower than 3.4% — but the unit economics work because cross-border travel spend is high-ticket (avg transaction $30–80) and high-frequency during trips.

Worked example: one user, one trip

A YouTrip user takes a 5-day trip to Tokyo. She spends ¥120,000 over 30 transactions (food, transport, shopping, one hotel). At a typical S$/¥ rate (~0.0089), that's S$1,068 of GTV.

YouTrip revenue on this trip:

  • Interchange (~1.3%): S$13.88
  • FX margin (~1.7%): S$18.16
  • Float (1 month at loaded balance ~S$1,500, ~3.5% APR / 12): ~S$4.38
  • Total: ~S$36.40 of revenue from one user, one trip.

If our CPApproved is S$15–17 (the SG target), this user's first trip alone already pays back her acquisition cost ~2.2× over. Every subsequent trip is gravy. This is why the business works.

How we measure CRM

The methodology that matters most here is iTV (incremental Transaction Volume) — the lift a CRM campaign creates above what would have happened anyway. The formula:

iTV = (Test TV per user − Control TV per user) × Test segment size

Every CRM campaign should have a control group (random hold-out who don't receive the message). After the journey window, we compare test vs. control on TV per user. If test > control with statistical significance (p < 0.05), the journey is incremental — meaning it caused additional spend that wouldn't have happened. If not, we kill or iterate.

Anchor concept

A push notification can correlate with spend (the user spent after receiving it) but that doesn't mean it caused the spend. A user about to travel might spend regardless. The control group is what separates "correlation" from "incremental." If you only remember one thing about CRM measurement: no control group, no causal claim.

9. What's actually hard right now

An onboarding doc that only tells you the success story is useless. Here are the genuine problems we wrestle with — knowing them gives you context for everything else.

  1. Defending SG market share against well-funded competitors. Wise is the #1 threat globally and is investing heavily in Singapore. They have a higher brand-trust ceiling because of their transparent-pricing positioning. Our defence is product breadth + Triple Zero clarity, but the moat isn't unassailable.
  2. Cracking AU and TH economics. SG works because we built the category. AU and TH are harder — different competitor landscape, different behaviour, different unit economics. Both markets are still in "find the working channel mix" mode.
  3. iOS attribution mess. Apple's SKAN framework deliberately blinds iOS attribution. Meta's reported iOS conversions consistently undercount by 21–47%. AppsFlyer is more trustworthy but still modelled. We have to triangulate constantly.
  4. Creative fatigue cycles. Every performance channel decays as audiences see the same creative repeatedly. We're on a perpetual treadmill of new hooks, new UGC, new KOL content, especially on Meta and TikTok.
  5. Power-law in users means power-law in risk. Top-decile users carry half the revenue — losing them concentration-risks the business. We have to retain heavy users specifically, not just "users in general."
  6. The future of card rails. Stablecoins, account-to-account rails (Singapore's PayNow + cross-border equivalents), and super-apps (Alipay+, LINE Pay's regional push) are all chipping at the long-term role of card networks. None of them threaten our take rate today — but the 5-year question is real.

3. The 4-week window — why we never look at one week alone

Every Monday report uses a 4-week comparison window: the reporting week (W0) plus the three prior weeks (W-1, W-2, W-3). Not one week, not five.

The reasoning is practical:

  • One week alone is noise. A holiday, a creative pause, a competitor's launch — any of these can swing a single week ±20% without telling you anything durable.
  • Three priors give you a trend. If W0 is bad, you can tell whether it's a one-week dip or a four-week slide. Those need very different responses.
  • Five weeks adds noise without adding signal. Creative cycles, audience refreshes, and seasonality usually play out inside 4 weeks. Going wider muddles the comparison.

You'll see this everywhere: every funnel table is 4 columns wide, every WoW delta is W0 vs. W-1, and any chart with more than 4 bars is probably a special-purpose deep-dive, not the weekly report.

2. Session token refresh — the #1 reason the report breaks

Metabase logs you in through Google OAuth + 2FA. The session lasts about 14 days, then it expires. When it expires, anything that talks to Metabase programmatically — including the helper scripts behind the Monday report — silently returns zero rows. Empty results, no error. Reports look fine until someone notices an entire channel is showing 0 approved.

The fix is a 60-second routine. Roughly:

How the token refresh works (you don't run this, but you should understand it)

  1. Open mtb.you.co in Chrome. Make sure you're logged in.
  2. Open DevTools (Cmd+Option+I). Go to the Network tab.
  3. Refresh the page. Click any request — /api/session/properties is reliable because it always fires on load.
  4. In Request Headers, find the Cookie line. Inside it, look for metabase.SESSION=<UUID>.
  5. Copy just the UUID (between = and the next ; if any).
  6. Save it to ~/.metabase_session on the machine running the report.
The common mistake

The session value is in the Cookie request header, not in a separate X-Metabase-Session header. The X-Metabase-Session header only appears on programmatic API calls from already-authenticated clients — not on the normal page-load request you'll be inspecting. When you see only a Cookie line, that's correct. Look inside it for metabase.SESSION=.

If a Monday report comes out with one channel reading 0 approved while spend looks normal, the first hypothesis is always "the session token expired." Refresh it and re-run.

3. The three Metabase Cards — one per market

Each market has one canonical Metabase Card for approved users. All three sit on top of the same underlying BigQuery view, VIEW_PERFORMANCE_MARKETING_ACQ_SSOT, which AppsFlyer feeds via a daily pipeline.

Card #MarketWhat it returns

What "approved by install date" means

The card returns approved users grouped by the install date, not the approval date. So a user who installed on Mon and got approved on Wed is counted on Mon. This matters because the metric is CPA on paid spend — you want to match the approval back to the day we spent the money that drove the install, not the day KYC happened to clear.

Consequence: the last 3 days of the card are always undercounted because some of those installs haven't been approved yet. By T+4 the numbers are stable; before T+4 they're descriptive only. Reports always flag the last 3 days as "incomplete — 72h attribution lag."

The 2000-row truncation gotcha

Metabase's /api/card/{id}/query endpoint silently caps results at 2000 rows. For wide date ranges (a quarter+) this can quietly drop the smallest-volume channel — usually ASA or Boost KOL — because their rows get truncated first while Meta and Google look fine. The Monday workflow defends against this in two ways: (a) when pulling a wide range, hit /api/dataset instead, which is uncapped; (b) always spot-check the smallest-volume channel — if it returns 0, truncation is the first hypothesis.

6. The twelve things that go wrong — and how to spot them

Every recurring failure mode has happened before. They've each cost hours when missed. Read these once now and again when something looks off in a report.

1Metabase session expired. Symptom: one channel reads 0 approved, spend looks fine. Fix: refresh ~/.metabase_session from DevTools cookie.
2Card 2000-row truncation. Symptom: smallest-volume channel (ASA, Boost KOL) shows 0 or weirdly low. Fix: pull from /api/dataset instead of /api/card/{id}/query, or chunk the date range to under 30 days at a time.
3iOS attribution lag. Symptom: last 3 days of approved look bad. This is structural — AppsFlyer SKAN postbacks settle over 72h. Always label "incomplete — 72h attribution lag." Never use action language on T-0 to T-3 data.
4Meta API default limit of 25. Symptom: ad-level spend looks 3× too low. Fix: always pass limit=500 on Meta ad-level pulls and verify the count returned is less than 500.
5SG ASA tracker column is wrong. Tracker column 22 has historically been about 30% of actual. Always use the ASA API live pull, never the tracker column.
6Google Ads SG billed in SGD. Symptom: SG Google Ads spend looks 35% too high. Fix: divide cost_micros by 1.35 to convert to USD. AU and TH Google Ads bill in their respective platform currencies (AUD and USD).
7AU tracker double-counted spend. Symptom: AU weekly spend ~A$10K higher than the platform totals add up to. This was a known bug; the rule now is: spot-check 2–3 tracker columns against platform APIs before any exec report.
8Currency in TH old sheets. Symptom: a TH spend number looks 30× bigger than it should. The platform bills USD; some older sheets were in THB. ÷31.5 to convert THB to USD if you see that.
9Partial-day data written to tracker. Symptom: today's date appears in a weekly total. Rule: never write a date range that includes today — the data is incomplete. Wait for the next morning.
10SG forgot to refresh the Overall section. Symptom: BAU Q43 changed but Overall Q84 didn't. Both sections of claude test weekly refresh together.
11OneSignal csv_export inflated by 16×. Not Monday-report-relevant directly but a useful reminder: bulk-export tools can return wildly wrong totals. Always spot-check against the dashboard before trusting bulk data.
12Wrong campaign-name regex. Symptom: a campaign nobody recognizes appears in a channel. Common causes: a campaign was renamed to drop "meta", or "Pangle" got missed in the TikTok regex. Fix: read the campaign list before trusting the channel total.

  1. Currency. Every USD figure is labelled USD; every native figure is labelled with the right symbol (S$, A$, ฿).
  2. GST. SG numbers have ×1.09 applied; AU and TH don't.
